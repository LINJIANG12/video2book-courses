Age. Students show their feelings differently at different ages. Let's take chronic fear, for example. Young children often regress when they are afraid over time. Their language skills might deteriorate, or they may even stop talking. They might have more tantrums, forget how to toilet train, become extra clingy, or even have nightmares.

Some children who are afraid might act out more and become aggressive, while others may withdraw more into themselves. Fearfulness in middle school and high school might be expressed through self-destructive and reckless behavior, excessive shame and guilt, fantasies of revenge, or sudden changes in their relationships.

Since our students' fears are expressed very differently than our own, we may not recognize that the cause of what we're seeing is fear, and not just bad behavior. Any unusual behavior in students should be a pause for concern.

Temperamental and personality differences. It's easy to gravitate toward students who are like us in personality. Introverts understand introverts better. Sensitive people connect with sensitivities in other people more easily. It is more important for us to stretch more to get to know different personalities, even when, and sometimes especially when, they annoy us. We also can recognize that some of the students we label as most difficult can sometimes actually be the most sensitive. They may be the ones who benefit most from the interventions and programs we offer.

Gender, culture, and power. Gender, culture, and power all work together in seamless ways to socialize our emotions. These forces might be adaptive in some circumstances, but in the long run, they also can create barriers to helping us understand the emotions of those around us.

For example, white, middle-class boys in many Western countries are already less emotionally expressive than girls by the time they're in preschool. Even which emotions they are allowed to express are different. Boys are allowed to express anger, contempt, and disgust, but are discouraged from more tender emotions like sadness, anxiety, and empathy. White girls, on the other hand, are encouraged to express happiness and nurturance, sympathy, and empathy, but they're socialized to suppress unpleasant emotions like sadness, fear, anxiety, shame, and guilt.

As we discussed in Section 5, race also plays a role. Studies show that inside their own families, African American parents have a strong social-emotional orientation. But outside their families, they socialize their children to have greater self-control and limit their emotional disclosures, compared to white parents. This is rooted in a history of oppression. It's not as safe for Black children to express anger or call up strong emotions in a conflict, since research studies and current events show they are punished disproportionately for expressing these emotions.

These are just two examples, but differences play out in all cultures. For adults, then, it is important that we take care to refrain from judging other people's emotions. It's also critical to proceed with sensitivity if we're expecting students to be more expressive. We may be opening them up to greater risk and to their own moral distress if we blunder across a cultural line.

It's important to take a lot of extra care to examine our own biases. What suppressions are we colluding in? Are we shutting down some people's feelings due to bias? Let's take a look at how inside feelings are different from outside behavior, using stress as an example.

Inside feelings of stress can show up in a lot of different ways on the outside. Remember, stress interferes with brain functioning. On the outside, a student under stress can appear to have academic problems. They may have trouble focusing, or they may not remember new material, or forget assignments. Their curiosity may be shut down, or they may not explore freely. Sometimes they can even become physically sick with stomachaches or headaches.

However, on the inside, the stress can actually be dampening down higher-order thinking processes in the prefrontal cortex. Attention, thinking, memory, and reasoning usually suffer when this happens. On the Mood Meter, their energy might be quite high, or the opposite, they could be quite inhibited, suffering from a freeze response.

Or, on the outside, stress can show up in students as emotion dysregulation. They may run hot and cold. They may be reactive or withdrawn. But on the inside, they just may be overwhelmed, trying to manage feelings like chronic fear or grief. They may have trouble maintaining a steady state. People who experience emotion dysregulation usually have problems with relationships because they're volatile, on a hair trigger, and deemed as being overly sensitive. Or again, at the other extreme, they may have the freeze reaction and appear to be numb, withdrawn, rigid, and not responsive.

The worst situation for a student is when they lack a reliably safe and supportive adult in their life. This is because students are wired to assess their security and safety by their connection to a steady adult figure. When that connection is absent, like when students are abused, or neglected, or in a chaotic situation, they're not only afraid, but they may feel badly about themselves.

On the outside, this can show up as a refusal to participate in class, or, on the other hand, perfectionism. Or, they might overcompensate for their low self-esteem by bragging or bullying others. On the inside, in these cases, they may feel a range of emotions from guilt, to shame, to helplessness.

Emotion self-regulation is the process of managing our own emotions. Emotion co-regulation is different. It refers to the ongoing process between two people, where one person's emotions are continuously influenced by the actions and emotional reactions of the other person.

In classrooms, this can be seen as a back-and-forth between the teacher and student, with the goal of supporting the child's emotions.

It also works between adults. Imagine that you and a colleague both hear upsetting news from your school or district leader. You both are feeling anxious. You walk up to your colleague, who appears to be more stressed than you are. You smile and offer a warm greeting. In response, her face lights up, her breathing relaxes, and she smiles back. In return, you feel calmer, and you say: don't worry, we'll figure this out.

In this example, co-regulation is about establishing a more positive state of mind between you and the other person. How does this work?

In the case of co-regulating with a child, just the presence of a caring adult can actually lower the levels of the stress hormone cortisol in the child. For this reason, a child can handle stress much better if they're with a person that makes them feel safe and secure. This person might be a parent, a teacher, a coach, a mentor, or another relative.

When adults support each other, or an adult supports a child, the stress hormone cortisol can decline during that moment of co-regulation. Also, the soothing hormone oxytocin and feel-good neurotransmitter dopamine can increase, all promoting feelings of well-being throughout the brain and body.

Our well-being depends a lot on our relationships. And the feelings that we're not alone, that our burdens are seen and shared, improve our ability to cope.

Co-regulation doesn't only apply to interactions between two people. Whole groups of people can be influenced, too. For example, a principal might try to shift the feelings of her entire staff to be more enthusiastic and motivated about going back to school, despite fears related to COVID-19. Or, a teacher may influence the emotions of students in her class because she wants to transition them from the high energy of recess to a lower energy required to focus on math.

Some adults try to command students to settle down, but that's really not co-regulation. A teacher who co-regulates with her students actually helps them to settle. For example, by speaking in friendly, but slightly softer and slower tones, acknowledging their feelings and energy, and moving to a brief mindfulness exercise, or checking in with their feelings before turning to the subject.

Why is it important to know about co-regulation? Humans are social creatures. We're always influencing each other's feelings, whether we realize it or not. To be able to do that with conscious awareness, intention, and skill is to wake up to a positive power, as well as to our responsibility to help others, especially those we love and care for.
